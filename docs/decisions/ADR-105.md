# ADR-105: Shortest periodic Euclidean distance on T²

- **Status:** Accepted by owner; metric specified, kernel and Gate 1 review OPEN
- **Decision date:** 2026-11-22
- **Selected option:** A (Batch 3, Decision 5)
- **Provenance:** Explicit project-owner selection in the PWFA conversation on 2026-11-22; NEW decision.
- **Related:** [ADR-102](ADR-102.md), [ADR-103](ADR-103.md), [ADR-104](ADR-104.md)

## Context and original alternatives

- **A — Shortest periodic Euclidean distance:** use Euclidean length of the shortest displacement among integer-lattice translates. **Selected; contemporaneous assistant recommendation.**
- **B — Periodic Manhattan distance:** sum shortest axis-aligned distances. Computationally accessible, but its neighborhoods are not Euclidean circles.
- **C — Learned distance metric:** adapt the distance geometry from data or memory, increasing flexibility but potentially conflating geometry and learned coupling.
- **Other:** Not selected.

## Owner decision and mathematical contract

With the previously approved normalized square flat torus T²=(R/Z)², define

`d_T([x],[y]) = min_{n in Z²} ||x-y+n||_2`,

where representatives x,y belong to R², [·] denotes their equivalence classes, and ||·||₂ is the ordinary Euclidean norm. Equivalently, in the unit-square coordinate representation, compute for each axis the minimum periodic displacement, then the Euclidean length of the two displacements.

This defines a continuous translation-invariant geodesic distance on the **unit square flat torus**, with maximum distance sqrt(2)/2. Multiple shortest displacements may exist at half-period boundaries, but their **distance** is unique. The metric need not be differentiable at cut loci; do not assume smoothness of a kernel composed with this distance without explicit hypotheses.

This choice does not specify the baseline kernel shape, coupling magnitude, learned-memory field, or numerical discretization.

## Contemporaneous recommendation, tradeoffs and obligations

Assistant recommended A, ranked B second and C third. Pros: distance respects periodic identification and has no preferred local axis; usable for distance-based base kernels. Cons: cut-locus nonsmoothness requires care for derivative-dependent claims. B produces diamond-shaped neighborhoods; C adds metric-learning assumptions and changes the separation of geometry and memory.

1. Prove metric well-definition on equivalence classes and triangle inequality, or cite independently checked mathematical derivations.
2. Specify kernel regularity at zero separation and cut loci before existence or error claims.
3. Distinguish globally valid torus symmetries from continuous Euclidean rotations.
4. Verify quadrature and numerical convergence once a discretization is selected.
5. Independent [Gate 1](../verification/review-gates.md) remains open.
